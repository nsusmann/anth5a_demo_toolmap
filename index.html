Discussion questions

Work through these with the tool open. Most ask you to set the filters a particular way and then say what you see — and, just as often, to say what the data cannot tell you.

1 · Getting oriented

  1. Set Technology to Lomekwian, then Oldowan, then Acheulean, one at a time. How does the number of techniques recorded per entry change across the three? What does that suggest about the pace of change over three million years? Technology filter → watch the Techniques column on the Table tab
  2. Compare the Oldowan chopper and the Acheulean biface photographs. Name two things a knapper had to do to make the handaxe that the chopper did not require. Filter by Technology to bring up the reference photographs

2 · Reading the evidence

  1. Bifacial retouch is recorded in none of the Lomekwian or Oldowan entries, and in most Acheulean ones. What does that single technique tell you about how a handaxe was shaped, and why is it a better dividing line than, say, the size of the tool? Chart → Technique frequency · Compare by → Technology
  2. Hard hammer percussion is recorded in all 155 entries — every single one. Is that because every toolmaker in three million years used a hard hammer, or is something about how the data was built producing that result? Chart → Technique frequency, no breakdown
  3. Find an entry with very few techniques recorded. Open it and read the Technology described field. Does the assemblage look genuinely simple, or does the publication just say very little about it? Table → sort by Techniques, ascending

3 · What the data cannot tell you

  1. A 0 in this dataset means “not reported,” not “did not happen.” Give an example from the tool where those two readings would lead you to opposite conclusions about a site.
  2. Filter to Lomekwian. There are two entries, both from Lomekwi 3. What evidence would you need before you were confident the Lomekwian is a distinct industry rather than one unusual site? Technology → Lomekwian
  3. Most early entries have a species attribution of Unattributed. Why would excavators decline to name a maker, and what does that caution cost us when we want to link a technology to a hominin species? Map → colour by Species, shape by Technology

4 · Space, time and the shape of the sample

  1. Country was not in the original dataset — it was added here from site coordinates. What is misleading about describing the Oldowan as coming from “Kenya, Ethiopia and Tanzania”? Chart → Entries by country
  2. Map the Oldowan, then the Acheulean. The Acheulean reaches well beyond Africa in this dataset and the Oldowan largely does not. Is that a real difference in how far the two technologies spread, or an artefact of which sites happen to be published here? Map → Technology filter, then Zoom to results
  3. This tool draws on a single publication. Name one region or one period you would expect to be badly under-represented, and say how you would check.

Remember that many techniques in this dataset were developed by Homo sapiens and fall outside the temporal scope of this course.

What is in the dataset

Each of the 155 rows is one technology or one set of technologies described in a published report, not one artefact and not one site. Where a paper described several distinct reduction sequences, each was coded separately and a further row records everything present across the whole assemblage — those summary rows are marked Whole assemblage in the table.

  • 145 archaeological entries, from 100 sites
  • 5 non-human primate tool-making behaviours
  • 5 technologies produced in controlled flintknapping experiments
  • 33 procedural units coded present/absent in every entry
  • Covers ~3.3 million years ago to the 19th century CE
  • Technology, Country and Region were added for this page and are not the authors' coding

Read the data carefully

  • “Technique” is not “tool type”. The dataset codes procedural units — the manufacturing steps a knapper took (faceting a platform, striking a burin spall, retouching a tang). It does not code named typologies such as “handaxe” or “Levallois point”. The Technology described field in the table often names those, in the coder's words.
  • Technology is not in the published dataset either — it is our reading. Only four of the 155 rows name an industry at all, in the coder's free text (“Oldowan” at EG12 and NY 18 Nyabusosi, “Acheulean handaxe” in one experiment). The rest of the Technology labels were assigned here from the site, its published age and the description of the artefacts. Probable marks the calls that are genuinely arguable: Olduvai Bed II BK, usually read as Developed Oldowan but coded here with no bifacial retouch; and Garba IVd, the contested ~1.7 Ma claim for an early Acheulean. Other is not “unknown” — it includes assemblages that do contain bifaces without being Acheulean, such as the Acheulo-Yabrudian at Qesem Cave, the Fauresmith at Kathu Pan 1, and Nor Geghi 1. Disagree with a call and you are doing archaeology correctly; the reasoning is in build/build_data.py.
  • Country is not in the published dataset. It was added here by looking up each site's coordinates against world boundaries. 19 sites sit just offshore of the coastline used and were assigned to the nearest country; these are flagged approx. Modern borders are also a poor fit for deep prehistory — treat country as a convenience for searching, not as an analytical unit.
  • Coordinates are approximate. The file used here has had site locations slightly displaced, so points show the right region but not an exact findspot.
  • Absence is not absence of evidence. A 0 means the technique was not reported in that publication. Richer, better-published assemblages tend to show more techniques, and reports tend to dwell on the showier pieces. As the authors note, common practices such as bipolar percussion are probably under-represented.
  • 10 entries have no coordinates (the primate and experimental entries) and so never appear on the map, though they do appear in charts and the table.
